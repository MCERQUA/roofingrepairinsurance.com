## Storm Season Creates Unique Insurance Risks

When a major hail event or hurricane hits, roofing repair and restoration contractors mobilize fast. Crew sizes triple. Operations cross state lines. Job volume spikes dramatically. And then the calls from insurance adjusters, property owners, and GCs start coming in for certificates.

This rapid scaling is exactly what makes storm damage roofing contracting so challenging to insure — and why so many storm roofers end up with inadequate coverage or get cancelled mid-season by carriers who didn't understand what they were writing.

## The Four Biggest Coverage Gaps for Storm Roofers

**1. Surge-capacity cancellation mid-season.**
Some carriers write a roofing policy based on a "normal" crew size, then cancel or non-renew when they see a sudden payroll spike from storm-season surge. This leaves contractors exposed at the worst possible time — when volume is highest and claims risk is elevated.

**2. Multi-state coverage gaps.**
If you follow a storm event from Texas into Louisiana, or deploy crews to Florida during hurricane season, your GL and workers' comp need to cover operations in all those states. Many basic policies have geographic limitations that create gaps for out-of-state surge operations.

**3. Inadequate completed-operations structure.**
High-volume storm repair creates significant completed-ops accumulation — hundreds of repair jobs in a short period, each carrying long-tail liability if a repair fails later. A standard GL with a narrow completed-ops sublimit is completely inadequate for the exposure.

**4. Workers' comp audit shock.**
Storm-season surge workers are often under-reflected in the estimated payroll at binding. When the annual audit comes in, the premium adjustment can be enormous — and unexpected. Without proper surge structuring, year-end audit bills routinely blindside storm contractors.

## How to Structure Insurance for Storm Restoration Work

**GL: Occurrence-based with proper completed-ops aggregate.** Storm repair creates dozens or hundreds of completed-ops exposures per season. Your completed-ops aggregate needs to be sized for the volume of work you do in a season — not a single residential project.

**Workers' comp: Surge-accommodation from day one.** The policy should be written with estimated payroll that reflects your peak-season size, not your off-season crew. Build in explicit language about surge capacity so your carrier understands seasonal swings and won't trigger cancellation when payroll jumps.

**Multi-state endorsements.** If you follow storms across state lines, your GL and workers' comp must explicitly cover each state you operate in. Workers' comp in particular is state-by-state — and penalties for operating without coverage in a state can be severe.

**Commercial auto: Hired and non-owned auto coverage.** Storm contractors often deploy personal vehicles from crew members during surge periods. Without hired and non-owned auto (HNOA), your company is exposed when those vehicles are in accidents on your business.

## The Cancellation Problem — and How to Solve It

The most dangerous moment in storm contracting isn't a claim — it's a mid-season cancellation. When a carrier that doesn't understand your business sees a surge in payroll or job count, they may cancel your policy as a risk management measure.

The solution is placing coverage with markets that specialize in, or have significant experience with, storm restoration contractors. These carriers understand seasonal volume swings, multi-state surge deployments, and the elevated claim frequency of post-storm work.

Working with an agency that knows storm contractor markets — and knows which carriers will stay with you through a busy season — is the difference between being covered and being exposed when it matters most.

## Documentation for Storm Restoration Claims

Storm restoration work generates a lot of paperwork — insurance adjuster estimates, scope of work agreements, material receipts. This same documentation is valuable for your insurance claims if something goes wrong.

Best practices:
- Keep a job file for each restoration project, including photos of pre-existing damage, scope-of-work agreements, and completion photos.
- Document crew assignments per job site so you can tie workers' comp claims to specific projects.
- Maintain a master log of completed restoration projects with completion dates — this is essential for managing completed-ops exposure.

## Bottom Line

Storm damage roofing contracting has a risk profile that demands purpose-built insurance — not a generic contractor GL and workers' comp that was never designed to accommodate surge capacity, multi-state operations, and high-volume completed-ops accumulation.
